1. Tiered AI access could potentially create “censorship through pricing,” but almost all intellectual property is priced to some extent: books cost money, journals (esp academic ones) have subscriptions, and software companies charge for products.
At what point does legitimate IP protection and charging for costly innovation become censorship? 
2. legally and procedurally, people supposedly learn and agree to what a platform does through long tedious Terms & Conditions before using it.
gap between formal consent and meaningful understanding: if the T&C is dozens of pages long, changes over time, and cannot realistically explain how a complex recommendation algorithm/LLM behave, what exactly have we consented to?
And if refusing means losing access to a platform that has become so essential in society, how meaningful is that consent in the first place?
3. a growing asymmetry identified in the book: LLMs make producing disinformation extremely cheap and scalable, while detection and fact-checking remain human-labor-intensive (should remain like this or not?).
If only automated detection can keep up with automated generation, but that 1) produces another arms race 2) also risk false positives and 3) gives opaque classifiers even more power over what people can say. So this “solution” could intensify exactly the algorithmic gatekeeping we are worried about.
* In addition, we want to separate misinformation from disinformation, but intent is nearly impossible to infer from content/even with context. For detection operate at the network/behavioral level, how do we distinguish a coordinated influence operation from legitimate coordinated behavior,
  and does doing so create an adversarial ML problem where attackers continually optimize generation strategies?
